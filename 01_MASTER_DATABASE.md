### Cyclic Sighing (Physiological Sigh Pattern)
**Protocol name:** Cyclic Sighing (Physiological Sigh Pattern)
**Primary purpose:** Rapidly reduce acute arousal and improve mood via exhale-emphasized double inhale + long exhale.
**Problem it targets:** Acute stress, elevated respiratory rate, low positive affect, need for a brief downshift.
**Typical duration:** 5 minutes daily; 1–3 cycles acutely
**Minimum useful duration:** 1–3 sighs (~30–60 seconds) for acute relief; 5 minutes for studied daily dose
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Sit or stand comfortably with upright posture.
2. Inhale slowly through the nose until lungs feel mostly full.
3. Without exhaling, take a second shorter inhale through the nose to maximally inflate the lungs.
4. Exhale slowly and fully through the mouth (or nose) until lungs empty.
5. Repeat continuously for ~5 minutes (or 1–3 cycles for acute use).
6. Return to normal breathing; notice body sensations without forcing calm.
**When to use it:** Acute stress spikes, pre-meeting nerves, end-of-day wind-down, or as a daily 5-min mood practice.
**Expected short-term effect:** Often reduced respiratory rate and improved positive affect within minutes to days of practice.
**Expected longer-term effect:** In a 28-day RCT, daily cyclic sighing improved mood more than matched mindfulness and reduced resting respiratory rate.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Double inhale recruits collapsed alveoli (physiological sigh); prolonged exhale increases parasympathetic influence via pulmonary stretch and vagal pathways; reduced chemoreflex drive may lower respiratory rate over time.
**Evidence level:** B
**Best supporting research:** Yilmaz Balban et al., Cell Reports Medicine 2023 (NCT05304000): remote RCT, daily 5-min cyclic sighing vs box breathing, cyclic hyperventilation, mindfulness; cyclic sighing best for positive affect and respiratory-rate reduction. PubMed 36630953; DOI 10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895. Note: HR/HRV did not significantly differ; healthy adults; remote delivery.
**Potential risks:** Lightheadedness if overdone; mouth-breathing dryness; may feel unusual initially.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Modify if severe respiratory disease, recent thoracic surgery, uncontrolled panic with breath-focus intolerance, or pregnancy-related breathing limits—use gentle single long exhales instead of forced maximal fills.
**Source links:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947/ ; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05304000
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Studied by Yilmaz Balban, Huberman, Spiegel et al. (Stanford); pattern based on known physiological sighing (respiratory neuroscience).

### Box Breathing (Square Breathing)
**Protocol name:** Box Breathing (Square Breathing)
**Primary purpose:** Stabilize attention and arousal with equal inhale–hold–exhale–hold timing.
**Problem it targets:** Performance anxiety, scattered attention, need for structured paced breathing under pressure.
**Typical duration:** 2–5 minutes (e.g., 4-4-4-4)
**Minimum useful duration:** ~1–2 minutes (4–8 cycles)
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Choose a count you can sustain without strain (commonly 4 seconds each side; some use 3–5).
2. Inhale through the nose for the count.
3. Hold lungs gently full for the same count (no straining).
4. Exhale through nose or mouth for the same count.
5. Hold empty (or near-empty) for the same count.
6. Repeat for 2–5 minutes; stop if dizzy.
**When to use it:** Pre-performance, between tasks, tactical stress contexts, brief reset.
**Expected short-term effect:** Subjective calm and attentional anchoring; may reduce state anxiety for some users.
**Expected longer-term effect:** In Balban 2023, box breathing improved mood/anxiety similarly to other groups but was not superior to cyclic sighing on primary mood/respiratory outcomes.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Paced breathing + mild breath holds provide cognitive structure (attentional load) and may modulate autonomic balance; equal counts are a convenience heuristic, not an individually optimized resonance rate.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Comparator arm in Yilmaz Balban et al. 2023 (Cell Rep Med). Broader paced-breathing literature supports slow breathing for HRV/stress more than the specific equal-hold box pattern. Limited high-quality trials isolating box breathing alone.
**Potential risks:** Breath holds can raise CO2/discomfort; dizziness; hypervigilance if counts are too long.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Avoid long holds if pregnancy, uncontrolled hypertension, panic disorder with breath-hold sensitivity, COPD/severe asthma. Shorten or remove holds.
**Source links:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/ ; Fincham et al. Sci Rep 2023 https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Popularized in tactical/performance settings (often linked to Navy SEAL training popularization; exact single inventor unclear).

### Coherent / Resonance Frequency Breathing (~6 breaths/min)
**Protocol name:** Coherent / Resonance Frequency Breathing (~6 breaths/min)
**Primary purpose:** Maximize respiratory sinus arrhythmia / HRV via slow paced breathing near ~0.1 Hz.
**Problem it targets:** Chronic stress load, low HRV, need for a trainable autonomic downshift practice.
**Typical duration:** 10–20 minutes; often ~5.5–6 breaths/min
**Minimum useful duration:** 5 minutes of comfortable slow breathing (~6/min)
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Sit upright, relaxed shoulders; breathe through the nose if comfortable.
2. Pace at about 6 breaths per minute (e.g., ~5 s inhale, ~5 s exhale) OR find personal resonance (~4.5–7/min) if using HRV guidance.
3. Prefer slightly longer exhale than inhale if that feels easier (common coaching cue).
4. Breathe gently—avoid overbreathing; if lightheaded, reduce depth.
5. Optional: visual/audio pacer; optional HRV biofeedback to maximize LF/RSA amplitude.
6. Practice daily; Lehrer protocols historically used ~20 min twice daily with biofeedback.
**When to use it:** Daily stress regulation, pre-sleep wind-down (if not too alerting), HRV training blocks, anxiety adjunct.
**Expected short-term effect:** Increased HRV/RSA during practice; often calmer mood and lower perceived stress.
**Expected longer-term effect:** HRV biofeedback / resonance breathing associated with small–moderate benefits for stress, some anxiety/depression, and BP in reviews; individual resonance may help but fixed ~6/min is often sufficient.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Near ~0.1 Hz, respiration, heart-rate oscillations, and baroreflex resonate, maximizing RSA and stimulating baroreflex gain (Lehrer/Vaschillo model).
**Evidence level:** B
**Best supporting research:** Lehrer & Gevirtz Frontiers Psychol 2014 PMC4104929; Steffen et al. 2017 resonance breathing HRV/BP/mood PMC5575449; Frontiers Neurosci 2020 practical guide to RF assessment. Newer work questions necessity of exact individual RF vs fixed 6/min.
**Potential risks:** Overbreathing (low CO2) causing dizziness/tingling; frustration with pacing; rare anxiety increase with interoceptive focus.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Severe respiratory disease; acute panic if breath focus worsens symptoms—use shorter sessions or therapist guidance. Capnometry advised in clinical HRVB when available.
**Source links:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4104929/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5575449/ ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2020.570400/full
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Paul Lehrer, Evgeny Vaschillo, and collaborators (HRV biofeedback / resonance frequency training).

### Diaphragmatic (Belly) Breathing
**Protocol name:** Diaphragmatic (Belly) Breathing
**Primary purpose:** Reduce accessory-muscle breathing and promote slower, deeper abdominal expansion for relaxation.
**Problem it targets:** Chest-dominant breathing under stress, somatic anxiety, tension.
**Typical duration:** 5–15 minutes
**Minimum useful duration:** 2–3 minutes of deliberate belly breathing
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Lie down or sit; one hand on chest, one on abdomen.
2. Inhale slowly through nose so the abdomen rises more than the chest.
3. Exhale slowly through nose or pursed lips, abdomen falling.
4. Keep shoulders relaxed; do not force maximal depth.
5. Continue 5–15 minutes; optionally combine with 6/min pacing.
**When to use it:** General anxiety reduction; physiotherapy adjunct for dysfunctional breathing; wind-down.
**Expected short-term effect:** Reduced muscle tension and subjective anxiety for many practitioners.
**Expected longer-term effect:** RCTs and reviews support diaphragmatic/slow breathing for anxiety symptoms with small–moderate effects; quality and protocols vary.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Reduced accessory muscle use, increased tidal volume at lower rate, possible vagal/parasympathetic facilitation, interoceptive attention shift.
**Evidence level:** B
**Best supporting research:** Diaphragmatic breathing RCTs for anxiety (e.g., Chen et al. related work); Fincham et al. 2023 breathwork meta-analysis (Sci Rep) small–moderate stress effects; technique heterogeneity is a limitation.
**Potential risks:** Hyperventilation if too deep/fast; abdominal discomfort; increased awareness of breath may worsen panic in some.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Acute abdominal pain/surgery; severe COPD may need clinician-guided breathing rehab rather than DIY max belly breathing.
**Source links:** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27553981/
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Longstanding clinical/physiotherapy practice; not a single inventor.

### 4-7-8 Breathing (as popularized by Weil)
**Protocol name:** 4-7-8 Breathing (as popularized by Weil)
**Primary purpose:** Use a counted inhale–hold–long-exhale pattern as a bedtime or acute calming cue.
**Problem it targets:** Difficulty falling asleep, acute anxiety, need for a simple memorable ratio.
**Typical duration:** 4 breath cycles (~1–2 minutes); repeat up to ~8 cycles if needed
**Minimum useful duration:** 4 cycles
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Place tongue tip behind upper front teeth (Weil cue; optional).
2. Exhale fully through mouth with a whoosh sound.
3. Close mouth; inhale quietly through nose for count of 4.
4. Hold breath for count of 7.
5. Exhale completely through mouth for count of 8.
6. Repeat for 4 cycles initially; do not exceed ~8 cycles when new.
**When to use it:** In bed when sleep onset is delayed; acute anxiety if holds are tolerated.
**Expected short-term effect:** May feel calmer via extended exhale and attentional focus; acute autonomic changes in some small studies.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Limited high-quality evidence specific to the exact 4-7-8 ratio; benefits likely overlap with slow breathing + prolonged exhale. Not a validated insomnia monotherapy.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Proposed: extended exhale and mild hypercapnia during hold; attentional distraction from rumination. Specific 4:7:8 timing is largely a mnemonic.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Sparse dedicated RCTs for Weil exact protocol; related paced/exhale-focused breathing has better support (Balban 2023; Lehrer HRVB). Small 4-7-8 studies show autonomic/anxiety changes—interpret cautiously.
**Potential risks:** Breath holds can cause discomfort, dizziness, or panic in sensitive people.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Skip or shorten holds in pregnancy, uncontrolled hypertension, panic with breath-hold aversion, or respiratory disease. Prefer cyclic sighing or 6/min breathing without holds.
**Source links:** Andrew Weil public educational materials (popularization); compare Balban 2023 and Lehrer HRVB literature.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Popularized by Andrew Weil, MD (pranayama-inspired counted breathing).

### Alternate Nostril Breathing (Nadi Shodhana / Anulom Vilom)
**Protocol name:** Alternate Nostril Breathing (Nadi Shodhana / Anulom Vilom)
**Primary purpose:** Traditional paced unilateral nostril breathing used for calm focus.
**Problem it targets:** Restlessness, desire for a structured traditional breathing ritual.
**Typical duration:** 5–10 minutes
**Minimum useful duration:** 3–5 minutes
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Sit upright. Thumb closes right nostril, ring finger closes left (classic; adaptations OK).
2. Close right; inhale left.
3. Close left; open right; exhale right.
4. Inhale right; switch; exhale left.
5. Continue 5–10 minutes at comfortable pace.
6. Optional equal counts; advanced retentions only with teacher guidance.
**When to use it:** Transition into meditation/focus work; evening downshift if calming.
**Expected short-term effect:** Often subjective calm and focused attention; some small studies show autonomic/HRV changes.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Evidence preliminary/mixed; many studies small or high bias risk. Treat as traditional with plausible paced-breathing benefits.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Traditional: balance of ida/pingala (not scientific). Scientific candidates: paced breathing effects, attentional control, mild unilateral nasal airflow differences (limited evidence).
**Evidence level:** D
**Best supporting research:** Small trials and yoga research reviews report anxiety/HRV changes; quality generally lower than Western paced-breathing RCTs.
**Potential risks:** Finger pressure discomfort; nasal congestion frustration; advanced breath holds add risk.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Significant nasal obstruction, active sinus infection, or breath-hold variants if contraindicated.
**Source links:** Traditional hatha yoga texts; modern small clinical/yoga studies (heterogeneous).
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Classical yoga tradition (hatha/pranayama lineages).

### Exhale-Emphasized Paced Breathing (No Holds)
**Protocol name:** Exhale-Emphasized Paced Breathing (No Holds)
**Primary purpose:** Simple calm breathing without holds: longer exhale than inhale.
**Problem it targets:** Need easy breathing tool when box holds or 4-7-8 holds are poorly tolerated.
**Typical duration:** 5 minutes
**Minimum useful duration:** 2 minutes
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Inhale nose ~3–4 seconds.
2. Exhale nose/mouth ~5–7 seconds.
3. No forced holds.
4. Keep breaths gentle.
5. Continue 5 minutes.
**When to use it:** Anxiety with hold intolerance; bridge to resonance breathing.
**Expected short-term effect:** Calm via extended exhale.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Consistent with Balban finding favoring exhale emphasis vs matched mindfulness.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Extended exhale / pulmonary stretch → parasympathetic bias hypotheses.
**Evidence level:** B
**Best supporting research:** Aligned with cyclic sighing emphasis (Balban 2023) and slow-breathing literature.
**Potential risks:** Minimal if gentle.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Severe respiratory disease—physiotherapist guidance.
**Source links:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Applied synthesis from paced-breathing research.

### Cyclic Hyperventilation with Retention (Caution Protocol)
**Protocol name:** Cyclic Hyperventilation with Retention (Caution Protocol)
**Primary purpose:** Inhale-emphasized cycling with short exhales and retention (studied comparator; not first-line calm tool).
**Problem it targets:** Sometimes sought for alertness/activation; used in some breathwork traditions.
**Typical duration:** 5 minutes (as in Balban 2023)
**Minimum useful duration:** Not recommended as default self-help for stress—prefer cyclic sighing.
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Only if medically appropriate and not seeking calm: relatively vigorous inhales with shorter passive exhales for cycles, with brief retention as instructed.
2. Stop immediately if dizziness, tingling, chest pain, or panic.
3. Prefer cyclic sighing / resonance breathing for stress/anxiety.
**When to use it:** Research context or carefully supervised activation—not for panic, insomnia, or hypertension.
**Expected short-term effect:** May increase arousal; in Balban 2023 still reduced anxiety vs baseline but was not best mood protocol.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Not superior to cyclic sighing for mood; hyperventilation-style practices carry more physiological risk.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Lowered CO2 (hypocapnia) alters cerebral blood flow; can create lightheadedness/tingling; retention adds CO2 rebound.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Comparator arm in Yilmaz Balban et al. 2023. Distinct from Wim Hof Method (separate evidence/risks).
**Potential risks:** Syncope, arrhythmia risk in susceptible people, panic, hypocapnia; never practice in water or while driving.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Cardiovascular disease, pregnancy, epilepsy, fainting history, bipolar mania risk, panic disorder—avoid. Not for children.
**Source links:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Operationalized in Balban/Huberman trial; related to cyclic hyperventilation traditions.

### Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR)
**Protocol name:** Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR)
**Primary purpose:** Systematically tense and release muscle groups to reduce somatic tension and anxiety.
**Problem it targets:** Muscle tension, somatic anxiety, sleep-onset difficulty related to bodily arousal.
**Typical duration:** 10–20 minutes (abbreviated) to 30–45 minutes (full)
**Minimum useful duration:** 5–10 minutes abbreviated (major groups only)
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Sit or lie down in a quiet place.
2. For each muscle group: tense gently 5–7 seconds, then release 20–30 seconds, noticing contrast.
3. Common order: hands/forearms → upper arms → forehead → eyes/cheeks → jaw → neck → shoulders → chest → abdomen → thighs → calves → feet.
4. Use only mild–moderate tension—never pain.
5. End with brief whole-body scan of residual tension.
**When to use it:** Evening wind-down, acute somatic anxiety, insomnia behavioral component (relaxation training).
**Expected short-term effect:** Reduced muscle tension and state anxiety for many users.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Meta-analyses support relaxation training including PMR for anxiety with moderate effects; AASM lists relaxation among behavioral insomnia options. Component network meta-analysis suggests relaxation alone may be less critical than sleep restriction/stimulus control for insomnia remission.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Learned discrimination of tension vs relaxation; reduced sympathetic/somatic arousal; attentional shift from worry to body sensations.
**Evidence level:** B
**Best supporting research:** Manzoni et al. BMC Psychiatry 2008 relaxation meta-analysis PMC2427027; Muhammad Khir et al. 2024 PMR systematic review PMC10844009; AASM 2021 behavioral insomnia guidelines.
**Potential risks:** Muscle strain if over-tensing; paradoxical anxiety; trauma survivors may find body focus activating.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Acute injuries; uncontrolled hypertension for strong isometric holds (use passive PMR); psychosis; severe trauma without support.
**Source links:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2427027/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10844009/
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Edmund Jacobson (Progressive Relaxation).

### Autogenic Training (AT)
**Protocol name:** Autogenic Training (AT)
**Primary purpose:** Self-generated passive concentration on heaviness/warmth and calm bodily states.
**Problem it targets:** Chronic tension, anxiety, psychosomatic stress symptoms.
**Typical duration:** 10–20 minutes per session; traditionally trained over weeks
**Minimum useful duration:** 5–10 minutes of heaviness/warmth formulas
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Sit or lie comfortably; eyes closed.
2. Silently repeat standard formulas with passive attention (e.g., My arms are heavy; My arms are warm; calm heartbeat/breathing; solar plexus warmth; forehead cool).
3. Do not force sensations; observe passively.
4. Practice regularly; traditionally learned with instructor for full protocol.
**When to use it:** Daily self-regulation; anxiety adjunct; some clinical AT programs.
**Expected short-term effect:** Warmth/heaviness sensations and subjective calm when training succeeds.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Systematic reviews suggest benefits for stress/anxiety; some meta-analytic estimates large but based on limited/heterogeneous trials—grade moderate-to-preliminary for general use.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Self-hypnosis-like passive concentration; learned autonomic self-regulation; expectancy and attentional control.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Seo & Kim 2019 AT stress meta-analysis PubMed 31477667; earlier AT reviews (Stetter & Kupper).
**Potential risks:** Anxiety increase in some; rare dissociation; not a substitute for medical care of somatic symptoms.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Acute psychosis; severe depression with passive suicidal ideation without care; trauma triggers with passive body focus.
**Source links:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31477667/
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Johannes Heinrich Schultz (1930s).

### Body Scan (MBSR Element)
**Protocol name:** Body Scan (MBSR Element)
**Primary purpose:** Systematically move attention through the body with nonjudgmental awareness.
**Problem it targets:** Mind wandering, tension unawareness, need for mindfulness anchor.
**Typical duration:** 10–45 minutes (MBSR classically ~45 min)
**Minimum useful duration:** 5–10 minutes abbreviated scan
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Lie down or sit; close eyes if comfortable.
2. Begin at feet (or head); notice sensations without trying to change them.
3. Move slowly region by region.
4. When mind wanders, note gently and return.
5. End with whole-body awareness for a few breaths.
**When to use it:** Mindfulness practice, wind-down, stress reduction programs.
**Expected short-term effect:** Increased body awareness; often mild relaxation; sometimes sleepiness.
**Expected longer-term effect:** MBSR as a package has moderate evidence for stress/anxiety/depression symptoms vs waitlist; isolated body-scan evidence thinner than full program.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Interoceptive attention training; decentering from rumination; possible autonomic calming secondary to sustained attention.
**Evidence level:** B
**Best supporting research:** MBSR evidence base (Kabat-Zinn); mindfulness meta-analyses for anxiety/depression. Attribute stronger evidence to MBSR package than stand-alone scan.
**Potential risks:** Trauma reactivation; frustration; sleepiness when driving after.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Active PTSD without trauma-sensitive guidance; acute mania; if lying down triggers panic—sit upright.
**Source links:** Kabat-Zinn MBSR curriculum; mindfulness clinical trial literature.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Jon Kabat-Zinn (MBSR formalization); vipassana-derived roots.

### Yoga Nidra / NSDR (Non-Sleep Deep Rest)
**Protocol name:** Yoga Nidra / NSDR (Non-Sleep Deep Rest)
**Primary purpose:** Guided rest inducing deep relaxation while typically remaining behaviorally awake; rest-without-requiring-sleep framing.
**Problem it targets:** High stress, recovery need when sleep is insufficient, structured rest; adjunct for insomnia symptoms (not proven sleep replacement).
**Typical duration:** 10–45 minutes guided audio
**Minimum useful duration:** 10 minutes guided body awareness + breath rest
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Lie down in a quiet safe place; use headphones with a reputable guided script.
2. Set intention/resolve if script includes sankalpa (optional traditional element).
3. Follow rotation of consciousness through body, breath, imagery as guided.
4. Remain still; falling asleep is OK—not a failure.
5. After ending cue, reorient slowly before standing.
6. Do NOT claim or expect this to replace nocturnal sleep physiological functions.
**When to use it:** Afternoon recovery, evening wind-down, high cognitive load days, insomnia adjunct under guidance.
**Expected short-term effect:** Reduced subjective stress/arousal; sometimes local EEG slow-wave features while scored awake; respiratory rate may fall.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Preliminary evidence for improved subjective sleep and some PSG metrics in small trials; mixed lab findings vs quiet rest. Promising but not equivalent to CBT-I or replacing sleep debt.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Guided attention + reduced sensory load may produce deep relaxation and local-sleep-like EEG features without whole-organism sleep. Traditional: yogic conscious sleep. Scientific claim of replacing sleep is not strongly supported.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Datta et al. NMJI 2021 (YN vs CBT-I, small RCT); local sleep EEG PMC9315270; PLOS One 2023 cognition/sleep novices; 2023 sleep-lab feasibility mixed vs quiet rest; EEG systematic reviews heterogeneous.
**Potential risks:** Dissociation; falling asleep in unsafe contexts; delaying medical insomnia care.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Narcolepsy diagnostic confusion; untreated severe OSA; trauma-sensitive needs—choose secular scripts. Not a substitute for prescribed sleep therapy.
**Source links:** https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0294678 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9315270/
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Modern Yoga Nidra systematized by Swami Satyananda Saraswati et al.; NSDR popularized by Andrew Huberman as umbrella label.

### Stimulus Control Therapy (CBT-I Component)
**Protocol name:** Stimulus Control Therapy (CBT-I Component)
**Primary purpose:** Re-associate bed/bedroom with sleep by eliminating incompatible bed behaviors and regulating sleep–wake contingencies.
**Problem it targets:** Chronic insomnia with conditioned arousal in bed (clock-watching, worry in bed).
**Typical duration:** Ongoing nightly rules (behavioral regimen), not a timed exercise
**Minimum useful duration:** Consistent application for ≥1–2 weeks to evaluate
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Use bed/bedroom only for sleep and sex (no work, scrolling, TV).
2. Go to bed only when sleepy.
3. If unable to sleep after ~15–20 minutes (rough estimate, avoid obsessive clock-watching), leave bed; do quiet non-stimulating activity in dim light; return when sleepy.
4. Repeat as needed.
5. Keep a fixed wake time every day including weekends.
6. Avoid daytime napping (or keep very brief/early if needed).
**When to use it:** Chronic insomnia disorder—ideally within full CBT-I under clinician guidance.
**Expected short-term effect:** May increase temporary sleepiness/frustration early while extinguishing conditioned arousal.
**Expected longer-term effect:** AASM supports stimulus control; 2024 component network meta-analysis finds stimulus control contributes to sleep quality/continuity improvements.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Classical/operant conditioning: extinguish bed–wake association; strengthen bed–sleep association; consolidate sleep drive with fixed wake time.
**Evidence level:** A
**Best supporting research:** AASM 2021 CPG for behavioral/psychological insomnia treatments PMC7853203; Edinger et al. systematic review; Furukawa et al. JAMA Psychiatry 2024 component network meta-analysis.
**Potential risks:** Temporary sleep loss; safety if drowsy driving; partner disruption.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Clinician guidance if bipolar spectrum (sleep loss mania risk), seizure disorders, high-risk occupations, pregnancy with medical complexity. Clinical protocol—not casual biohacking.
**Source links:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/ ; https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2814164
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Richard Bootzin (stimulus control therapy for insomnia).

### Sleep Restriction Therapy (CBT-I Component)
**Protocol name:** Sleep Restriction Therapy (CBT-I Component)
**Primary purpose:** Temporarily limit time in bed to match average sleep time, consolidating sleep, then titrate upward.
**Problem it targets:** Chronic insomnia with low sleep efficiency (long time in bed, fragmented sleep).
**Typical duration:** Multi-week clinician-guided titration
**Minimum useful duration:** Requires baseline sleep diary ≥1 week before starting
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Track sleep diary (time in bed, estimated total sleep).
2. Set time-in-bed window ≈ average total sleep time (often with a floor, e.g., ≥5–5.5 h—clinician sets).
3. Fix wake time; derive bedtime from window.
4. No additional time in bed outside window.
5. When sleep efficiency improves (often ≥85–90% for a week), extend time in bed by ~15–20 minutes.
6. Continue titration until optimal consolidation with adequate duration.
**When to use it:** Chronic insomnia—strongly prefer trained CBT-I clinician.
**Expected short-term effect:** Increased sleepiness, irritability, cognitive slowing early—expected and monitored.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Among the most potent CBT-I components for sleep continuity/efficiency (network meta-analysis).
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Increases homeostatic sleep pressure; reduces excess time awake in bed; consolidates sleep.
**Evidence level:** A
**Best supporting research:** AASM 2021 guideline; Furukawa et al. JAMA Psychiatry 2024 cNMA—sleep restriction among critical components. ACP CBT-I guideline context.

### Caffeine Timing Cutoff
**Protocol name:** Caffeine Timing Cutoff
**Primary purpose:** Reduce caffeine-related sleep disruption by stopping intake sufficiently before bedtime.
**Problem it targets:** Sleep latency/continuity problems with afternoon/evening caffeine.
**Typical duration:** Policy: no caffeine within 8–10+ hours of bedtime (individualize)
**Minimum useful duration:** Avoid caffeine after early afternoon if sensitive

### WOOP (Mental Contrasting + Implementation Intentions)
**Protocol name:** WOOP (Mental Contrasting + Implementation Intentions)
**Primary purpose:** Increase goal attainment by contrasting wished future with real obstacles, then forming if–then plans.
**Problem it targets:** Fantasy-only goal setting that fails on contact with reality; motivation without strategy.
**Typical duration:** 5–15 minutes per goal
**Minimum useful duration:** 5 minutes for one wish
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Wish: choose a meaningful, challenging, feasible wish.
2. Outcome: vividly imagine the best outcome and how it would feel.
3. Obstacle: identify the main inner obstacle (habit, emotion, belief)—be honest.
4. Plan: form If [obstacle], then I will [action].
5. Repeat for key goals; revisit weekly.
**When to use it:** Personal/health/academic/work goals; de-biasing pure positive visualization.
**Expected short-term effect:** Clearer commitment when expectancy is adequate; sometimes decreased commitment if goal is unrealistic (feature, not bug).
**Expected longer-term effect:** MCII meta-analyses show small–moderate benefits for goal attainment; useful low-cost strategy.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Mental contrasting energizes goal pursuit when expectations are sufficient by linking future to present obstacle; II automates obstacle response (Oettingen + Gollwitzer).
**Evidence level:** A
**Best supporting research:** Oettingen mental contrasting; WOOP practicalization; Frontiers Psychology 2021 MCII meta-analysis (g≈0.34; trim-and-fill lower). Health behavior MCII meta-analyses similar.
**Potential risks:** Demotivation if wish is fantasy-only with low expectancy—may correctly reduce pursuit.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Not for acute suicidality/crisis planning—seek professional care.
**Source links:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/full
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Gabriele Oettingen (mental contrasting/WOOP); II with Peter Gollwitzer.

### SMART Goals with Evidence Caveats
**Protocol name:** SMART Goals with Evidence Caveats
**Primary purpose:** Structure goals as Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound—while recognizing SMART is a heuristic, not a magic formula.
**Problem it targets:** Vague goals; also over-constraining exploratory/creative aims.
**Typical duration:** 10–20 minutes planning
**Minimum useful duration:** Rewrite one vague goal into a testable next action
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Write the goal specifically (what/when/where).
2. Define measurement and a check-in date.
3. Reality-check achievability with constraints.
4. Confirm relevance to values (avoid inherited shoulds).
5. Set time bound and first if–then plan.
6. Caveat: for learning/creative goals, pair with process metrics, not only outcome KPIs.
**When to use it:** Project planning, team OKRs translation to personal actions.
**Expected short-term effect:** Clarity and coordination.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Organizational practice support; experimental superiority of SMART acronym vs other frameworks is not strongly established as unique—implementation quality matters more.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Reduces ambiguity; enables feedback; may increase commitment via concreteness.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** SMART popular management heuristic (Doran 1981 often cited). Goal-setting theory (Locke & Latham) has stronger evidence for specific difficult goals + feedback than for the SMART acronym per se.
**Potential risks:** False precision; neglecting autonomy/mastery; demotivating impossible A.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Using SMART to micromanage recovery from depression—prefer values + tiny actions with clinician/coach.
**Source links:** Locke & Latham goal-setting theory reviews; Doran SMART note.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** George T. Doran (SMART popularization); Locke & Latham (goal-setting science).

### Habit Stacking (Routine-Based Cueing)
**Protocol name:** Habit Stacking (Routine-Based Cueing)
**Primary purpose:** Attach a new tiny behavior to an existing reliable routine cue.
**Problem it targets:** Forgetting new habits; weak cues.
**Typical duration:** 2–5 minutes to design; seconds to perform
**Minimum useful duration:** One stack: After [current habit], I will [new habit <2 min].
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Identify a stable daily routine (make coffee, brush teeth).
2. Attach a tiny version of the new habit immediately after.
3. Keep the new habit laughably small initially.
4. Increase only after automaticity grows.
5. Label: popularized by James Clear; scientifically closest to implementation intentions / habit cue research.
**When to use it:** Building keystone micro-habits.
**Expected short-term effect:** Higher consistency if cue is truly stable.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Habit formation depends heavily on repetition in stable contexts; stack type (routine vs time) may matter less than consistent repetition (RCT evidence).
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Cue–behavior association; reduces prospective memory load.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Popular science (Atomic Habits). Underlying science: implementation intentions; Wood/Neal habit research; RCTs on routine- vs time-based cue planning.
**Potential risks:** Stacking onto unstable cues; ego inflation from reading without practicing.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Relying on books alone for clinical behavior change needs.
**Source links:** Habit formation RCTs; Gollwitzer II literature; Clear as popularizer.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Popularized by James Clear; related ideas in behavior analysis and BJ Fogg Tiny Habits.

### TIPP (DBT Crisis Survival Skill)
**Protocol name:** TIPP (DBT Crisis Survival Skill)
**Primary purpose:** Rapidly change body chemistry to reduce extreme emotional arousal.
**Problem it targets:** Emotional crisis, urge surfing need, acute overwhelm.
**Typical duration:** 5–15 minutes
**Minimum useful duration:** 1 TIPP element for 30–60 seconds (e.g., cold water)
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Temperature: cold water on face / cold pack / brief cold shower (dive-reflex informed); caution with heart conditions.
2. Intense exercise: short burst (e.g., 20–60 sec jumping jacks) if safe.
3. Paced breathing: slow exhale-emphasized breathing.
4. Progressive relaxation: paired muscle relaxation.
5. Use as bridge to later problem-solving—not as only long-term strategy.
**When to use it:** Emotional intensity 8–10/10; before destructive impulses.
**Expected short-term effect:** Often reduced peak arousal within minutes.
**Expected longer-term effect:** DBT skills training packages have evidence for emotion dysregulation populations; isolated TIPP RCTs limited—clinically established within DBT.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Cold face stimulation can trigger mammalian dive reflex (parasympathetic); exercise metabolizes arousal; breathing/PMR reduce somatic tension.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Linehan DBT manuals; evidence strongest for DBT programs overall, not each micro-skill in isolation. Physiological rationale for cold/dive reflex is plausible.
**Potential risks:** Cold water + cardiac issues; intense exercise injury; misuse as chronic avoidance of therapy.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Cardiovascular disease—modify Temperature; eating disorders—careful with exercise; seek emergency care for safety risk.
**Source links:** DBT skills training handouts (Linehan); clinical DBT outcome literature.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Marsha Linehan / DBT skills framework.

### Opposite Action (DBT Skill — Not Full Therapy)
**Protocol name:** Opposite Action (DBT Skill — Not Full Therapy)
**Primary purpose:** Act opposite to emotion-driven urges when emotion is unjustified or unhelpful in intensity/duration.
**Problem it targets:** Emotion-consistent actions that worsen problems (avoid when fear unjustified; isolate when sad).
**Typical duration:** Minutes to hours depending on action
**Minimum useful duration:** One small opposite action in the next 10 minutes
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Name the emotion and the urge.
2. Check the facts: is the emotion justified by evidence? Is intensity matching?
3. If emotion/urge is not justified or not effective, choose opposite action (e.g., approach when fear unjustified; gentle activity when sadness urges withdrawal).
4. Do opposite action all the way (posture, expression, thoughts aligned).
5. This is a skill excerpt—not delivery of full DBT therapy.
**When to use it:** When emotions drive ineffective behavior and facts do not fully support the urge.
**Expected short-term effect:** Reduced emotion intensity after opposite action for many.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Component of DBT emotion regulation; package evidence > isolated skill trials.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Breaks emotion–action feedback loops; behavioral exposure-like effects when approaching avoided cues.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** DBT skills manuals; emotion regulation research on behavioral opposite strategies.
**Potential risks:** Opposite action can be wrong if emotion is justified (e.g., fear of real danger)—always check facts first.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Safety threats, abuse situations, or medical danger—do not opposite-action away from real risk.
**Source links:** Linehan DBT Emotion Regulation skills.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Marsha Linehan (DBT).

### Cognitive Reappraisal
**Protocol name:** Cognitive Reappraisal
**Primary purpose:** Change emotional impact by changing interpretation of a situation.
**Problem it targets:** Catastrophic appraisals, anger/anxiety amplification via meaning-making.
**Typical duration:** 3–10 minutes
**Minimum useful duration:** One alternative appraisal written down
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Identify the situation and automatic appraisal (This means I am doomed).
2. Generate alternative appraisals that are realistic (not toxic positivity).
3. Ask: evidence for/against? Other explanations? What would I tell a friend?
4. Choose a balanced reappraisal and note how body emotion shifts.
5. If stuck in deep belief structures, use full CBT with a clinician.
**When to use it:** Anxiety/anger spikes driven by interpretation; post-event processing.
**Expected short-term effect:** Reduced negative emotion intensity when reappraisal succeeds.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Core CBT/emotion regulation strategy with substantial laboratory and clinical support (Gross process model; CBT trials).
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Prefrontal modulation of limbic responses via meaning change (proposed); reduces secondary elaborative processing.
**Evidence level:** A
**Best supporting research:** James Gross emotion regulation framework; CBT outcome literature; experimental reappraisal studies.
**Potential risks:** Intellectualization avoiding needed action; invalidating real injustice with forced reappraisal.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Acute trauma processing needs specialized care; psychosis—reality testing with professionals.
**Source links:** Gross emotion regulation reviews; CBT clinical guidelines.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Formalized in emotion regulation science by James Gross; clinical roots in CBT (Beck et al.).

### Affect Labeling (Putting Feelings into Words)
**Protocol name:** Affect Labeling (Putting Feelings into Words)
**Primary purpose:** Reduce emotional reactivity by specifically naming felt emotions.
**Problem it targets:** Diffuse distress, amygdala-driven reactivity, alexithymic vagueness.
**Typical duration:** 1–3 minutes
**Minimum useful duration:** One precise label (I feel angry and ashamed)
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Pause; notice body cues.
2. Choose specific emotion words (not just bad/fine).
3. Say or write: I am noticing anxiety/sadness/anger…
4. Optionally rate intensity 0–10.
5. Then choose a follow-up skill (breath, reappraisal, opposite action) if needed.
**When to use it:** Rising emotion; conflict; journaling; panic early signs.
**Expected short-term effect:** Often dampened limbic reactivity; subjective relief varies.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Supports emotional granularity associated with better regulation in observational/experimental work.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Lieberman et al.: affect labeling increases right vlPFC activity and reduces amygdala responses—implicit emotion regulation.
**Evidence level:** B
**Best supporting research:** Lieberman et al. 2007 Psychological Science Putting Feelings Into Words (PubMed 17576282); Torre & Lieberman reviews on affect labeling.
**Potential risks:** In some high-reactivity contexts labeling alone may be insufficient or inconsistently helpful.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Not a standalone treatment for PTSD/clinical depression.
**Source links:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576282/
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Research program associated with Matthew Lieberman and colleagues.

### Cold Face Stimulation (Dive-Reflex Informed)
**Protocol name:** Cold Face Stimulation (Dive-Reflex Informed)
**Primary purpose:** Acutely increase parasympathetic tone via cold stimulus to face.
**Problem it targets:** Peak emotional arousal, anger/panic spike (TIPP Temperature element).
**Typical duration:** 30–60 seconds
**Minimum useful duration:** 15–30 seconds
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Bend forward; apply cold water or cold pack to face especially forehead/eyes/cheeks.

4. Stop if pain or cardiac symptoms.
**When to use it:** Emotional crisis skill; not a required daily biohack.

**Potential risks:** Arrhythmia risk in susceptible; cold shock; unsafe if epilepsy/heart disease.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Cardiac disease, eating disorders with cold purging patterns, unsupervised breath-hold in water.
**Source links:** DBT TIPP; dive reflex physiology references.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** DBT TIPP Temperature skill; dive reflex science older.
