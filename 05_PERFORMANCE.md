### Box Breathing (Square Breathing)
**Protocol name:** Box Breathing (Square Breathing)
**Primary purpose:** Stabilize attention and arousal with equal inhale–hold–exhale–hold timing.
**Problem it targets:** Performance anxiety, scattered attention, need for structured paced breathing under pressure.
**Typical duration:** 2–5 minutes (e.g., 4-4-4-4)
**Minimum useful duration:** ~1–2 minutes (4–8 cycles)
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Choose a count you can sustain without strain (commonly 4 seconds each side; some use 3–5).
2. Inhale through the nose for the count.
3. Hold lungs gently full for the same count (no straining).
4. Exhale through nose or mouth for the same count.
5. Hold empty (or near-empty) for the same count.
6. Repeat for 2–5 minutes; stop if dizzy.
**When to use it:** Pre-performance, between tasks, tactical stress contexts, brief reset.
**Expected short-term effect:** Subjective calm and attentional anchoring; may reduce state anxiety for some users.
**Expected longer-term effect:** In Balban 2023, box breathing improved mood/anxiety similarly to other groups but was not superior to cyclic sighing on primary mood/respiratory outcomes.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Paced breathing + mild breath holds provide cognitive structure (attentional load) and may modulate autonomic balance; equal counts are a convenience heuristic, not an individually optimized resonance rate.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Comparator arm in Yilmaz Balban et al. 2023 (Cell Rep Med). Broader paced-breathing literature supports slow breathing for HRV/stress more than the specific equal-hold box pattern. Limited high-quality trials isolating box breathing alone.
**Potential risks:** Breath holds can raise CO2/discomfort; dizziness; hypervigilance if counts are too long.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Avoid long holds if pregnancy, uncontrolled hypertension, panic disorder with breath-hold sensitivity, COPD/severe asthma. Shorten or remove holds.
**Source links:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/ ; Fincham et al. Sci Rep 2023 https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Popularized in tactical/performance settings (often linked to Navy SEAL training popularization; exact single inventor unclear).

### Coherent / Resonance Frequency Breathing (~6 breaths/min)
**Protocol name:** Coherent / Resonance Frequency Breathing (~6 breaths/min)
**Primary purpose:** Maximize respiratory sinus arrhythmia / HRV via slow paced breathing near ~0.1 Hz.
**Problem it targets:** Chronic stress load, low HRV, need for a trainable autonomic downshift practice.
**Typical duration:** 10–20 minutes; often ~5.5–6 breaths/min
**Minimum useful duration:** 5 minutes of comfortable slow breathing (~6/min)
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Sit upright, relaxed shoulders; breathe through the nose if comfortable.
2. Pace at about 6 breaths per minute (e.g., ~5 s inhale, ~5 s exhale) OR find personal resonance (~4.5–7/min) if using HRV guidance.
3. Prefer slightly longer exhale than inhale if that feels easier (common coaching cue).
4. Breathe gently—avoid overbreathing; if lightheaded, reduce depth.
5. Optional: visual/audio pacer; optional HRV biofeedback to maximize LF/RSA amplitude.
6. Practice daily; Lehrer protocols historically used ~20 min twice daily with biofeedback.
**When to use it:** Daily stress regulation, pre-sleep wind-down (if not too alerting), HRV training blocks, anxiety adjunct.
**Expected short-term effect:** Increased HRV/RSA during practice; often calmer mood and lower perceived stress.
**Expected longer-term effect:** HRV biofeedback / resonance breathing associated with small–moderate benefits for stress, some anxiety/depression, and BP in reviews; individual resonance may help but fixed ~6/min is often sufficient.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Near ~0.1 Hz, respiration, heart-rate oscillations, and baroreflex resonate, maximizing RSA and stimulating baroreflex gain (Lehrer/Vaschillo model).
**Evidence level:** B
**Best supporting research:** Lehrer & Gevirtz Frontiers Psychol 2014 PMC4104929; Steffen et al. 2017 resonance breathing HRV/BP/mood PMC5575449; Frontiers Neurosci 2020 practical guide to RF assessment. Newer work questions necessity of exact individual RF vs fixed 6/min.
**Potential risks:** Overbreathing (low CO2) causing dizziness/tingling; frustration with pacing; rare anxiety increase with interoceptive focus.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Severe respiratory disease; acute panic if breath focus worsens symptoms—use shorter sessions or therapist guidance. Capnometry advised in clinical HRVB when available.
**Source links:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4104929/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5575449/ ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2020.570400/full
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Paul Lehrer, Evgeny Vaschillo, and collaborators (HRV biofeedback / resonance frequency training).

### Cyclic Hyperventilation with Retention (Caution Protocol)
**Protocol name:** Cyclic Hyperventilation with Retention (Caution Protocol)
**Primary purpose:** Inhale-emphasized cycling with short exhales and retention (studied comparator; not first-line calm tool).
**Problem it targets:** Sometimes sought for alertness/activation; used in some breathwork traditions.
**Typical duration:** 5 minutes (as in Balban 2023)
**Minimum useful duration:** Not recommended as default self-help for stress—prefer cyclic sighing.
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Only if medically appropriate and not seeking calm: relatively vigorous inhales with shorter passive exhales for cycles, with brief retention as instructed.
2. Stop immediately if dizziness, tingling, chest pain, or panic.
3. Prefer cyclic sighing / resonance breathing for stress/anxiety.
**When to use it:** Research context or carefully supervised activation—not for panic, insomnia, or hypertension.
**Expected short-term effect:** May increase arousal; in Balban 2023 still reduced anxiety vs baseline but was not best mood protocol.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Not superior to cyclic sighing for mood; hyperventilation-style practices carry more physiological risk.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Lowered CO2 (hypocapnia) alters cerebral blood flow; can create lightheadedness/tingling; retention adds CO2 rebound.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Comparator arm in Yilmaz Balban et al. 2023. Distinct from Wim Hof Method (separate evidence/risks).
**Potential risks:** Syncope, arrhythmia risk in susceptible people, panic, hypocapnia; never practice in water or while driving.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Cardiovascular disease, pregnancy, epilepsy, fainting history, bipolar mania risk, panic disorder—avoid. Not for children.
**Source links:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Operationalized in Balban/Huberman trial; related to cyclic hyperventilation traditions.

### Morning Outdoor Light Timing
**Protocol name:** Morning Outdoor Light Timing
**Primary purpose:** Anchor circadian phase with bright morning light exposure.
**Problem it targets:** Delayed sleep phase tendency, morning grogginess, jet-lag eastward prep support.
**Typical duration:** 10–30 minutes outdoor light soon after waking
**Minimum useful duration:** 5–10 minutes on a bright morning (longer if overcast)
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Within ~30–60 minutes of waking, go outdoors without sunglasses if safe (do not stare at sun).
2. Face general outdoor ambient light; cloudy still helps vs indoor.
3. Combine with movement if desired.
4. In evening, dim indoor lights to avoid delaying circadian phase.
5. For jet lag: timing depends on direction/phase—use careful schedules.
**When to use it:** Daily circadian anchoring; seasonal low light; mild DSPS tendencies.
**Expected short-term effect:** Increased alertness; morning melatonin suppression supports wake consolidation.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Regular morning light helps stabilize circadian timing; clinical chronotherapy uses timed light for circadian rhythm disorders (AASM parameters).
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (melanopsin) signal SCN; morning light advances/stabilizes phase depending on prior phase.
**Evidence level:** A
**Best supporting research:** Circadian photoreception science; AASM practice parameters for circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders; jet-lag intervention reviews (Frontiers Physiol 2019).
**Potential risks:** UV skin/eye risk—use sensible outdoor practice; manic precipitation rare in bipolar with very bright light therapy boxes—medical supervision for clinical light boxes.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Ocular conditions per ophthalmologist; bipolar—coordinate timed bright light with clinician.
**Source links:** https://aasm.org/resources/practiceparameters/pp_circadianrhythm.pdf ; https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2019.00927
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Circadian biology / sleep medicine field.

### Brief Nap Protocol (10–20 min)
**Protocol name:** Brief Nap Protocol (10–20 min)
**Primary purpose:** Reduce sleepiness and restore alertness without deep-sleep inertia.
**Problem it targets:** Afternoon sleepiness, performance dip, partial sleep restriction recovery assist.
**Typical duration:** 10–20 minutes
**Minimum useful duration:** ~10 minutes if very sleepy (set alarm)
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Nap early afternoon when possible (circadian nap window).
2. Set alarm for 10–20 minutes; dark cool place.
3. Optional caffeine-nap: caffeine immediately before nap so it kicks in on waking (practical; evidence mixed).
4. Avoid 60–90+ min naps if inertia or night insomnia is a problem.
5. If chronic insomnia, avoid naps per stimulus control unless clinician allows.
**When to use it:** Sleep-restricted days, shift workers (with care), students—avoid if it ruins night sleep.
**Expected short-term effect:** Improved alertness and cognitive performance for many after brief naps.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Habitual long daytime sleep can fragment nocturnal sleep in insomnia; brief naps are a tool, not a fix for chronic sleep debt.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Reduce homeostatic sleep pressure partially; avoid slow-wave heavy sleep that causes inertia.
**Evidence level:** B
**Best supporting research:** Nap/performance literature in sleep science; operational studies support brief naps. Insomnia guidelines often discourage naps.
**Potential risks:** Sleep inertia if longer; nocturnal insomnia worsening.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Chronic insomnia under stimulus control; untreated severe OSA; immediately before high-risk driving if inertia risk.
**Source links:** Sleep research reviews on napping and performance; AASM insomnia guidance on naps.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** N/A (operational sleep science practice).

### Moderate Aerobic Exercise Bout
**Protocol name:** Moderate Aerobic Exercise Bout
**Primary purpose:** Reduce stress/anxiety/depressive symptoms via planned aerobic activity.
**Problem it targets:** Chronic stress, low mood, anxiety symptoms, sedentary load.
**Typical duration:** 20–40 minutes moderate intensity
**Minimum useful duration:** 10–15 minutes brisk movement if that is all available
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Choose sustainable modality (brisk walk, cycle, swim, jog).
2. Warm up 3–5 min.
3. Work at moderate intensity (can talk but not sing) 20–40 min.
4. Cool down; hydrate.
5. Aim most days/week for chronic benefits; even single bouts help acute mood for many.
**When to use it:** Chronic stress management; acute mood lift; foundational health behavior.
**Expected short-term effect:** Often improved mood/anxiety within minutes to hours post-exercise.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Meta-analyses support exercise for depressive symptoms; anxiety benefits present but sometimes smaller/less consistent vs active controls.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Multiple: monoamines, neurotrophic factors (e.g., BDNF hypotheses), HPA modulation, thermogenic, self-efficacy, sleep improvement.
**Evidence level:** A
**Best supporting research:** Cochrane exercise for depression reviews; newer umbrella/meta-analyses of exercise for depression/anxiety. Adjunct, not always monotherapy for major clinical disorders.
**Potential risks:** Injury; cardiac events in high-risk unscreened individuals; overtraining.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Medical clearance if cardiovascular risk, acute injury, eating-disorder exercise compulsion.
**Source links:** Cochrane Library exercise-depression reviews; recent exercise–anxiety/depression meta-analyses.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** N/A (exercise physiology / psychiatry evidence synthesis).

### Pre-Performance Routine (PPR)
**Protocol name:** Pre-Performance Routine (PPR)
**Primary purpose:** Stabilize attention and arousal before a skilled performance via a consistent short sequence.
**Problem it targets:** Choking under pressure, variable warm-up, distracted start.
**Typical duration:** 30–120 seconds immediately before execution
**Minimum useful duration:** 15–30 seconds consistent cue sequence
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Define 3–5 steps you can repeat identically (e.g., breath → cue word → visual target → physical set).
2. Include one arousal tool (e.g., physiological sigh or box breath) and one attentional cue (external focus preferred for many motor skills).
3. Practice the routine in low-stakes settings until automatic.
4. Use the same routine in competition/high stakes.
5. Evaluate and simplify if it becomes over-controlling.
**When to use it:** Sports, music, presentations, procedural skills, exams.
**Expected short-term effect:** Reduced performance variability; better attentional set.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Sport psychology research supports PPRs for consistency under pressure.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Attentional control; reduced irrelevant cognitions; arousal regulation; motor preparation consistency.
**Evidence level:** B
**Best supporting research:** Sport psychology reviews on pre-performance routines; experimental studies under pressure. Effects vary by sport/skill and routine quality.
**Potential risks:** Superstition/compulsion if overly rigid; time-wasting complexity.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** If routine morphs into OCD-like compulsion—simplify with sport psychologist.
**Source links:** Applied sport psychology literature on routines under pressure.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Applied sport psychology field practice.

### Centering (Ravizza-Style)
**Protocol name:** Centering (Ravizza-Style)
**Primary purpose:** Rapidly shift from distracted/tense state to controlled, present, process-focused state.
**Problem it targets:** Pre-competition nerves, emotional carryover, loss of composure.
**Typical duration:** 30–90 seconds
**Minimum useful duration:** 1 deliberate breath + focal cue
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Recognize tension/distraction (awareness).
2. Pick a physical focal point (e.g., podium edge).
3. Diaphragmatic breath; release shoulders/jaw on exhale.
4. Use a short process cue (see seams, smooth start) not outcome (win).
5. Commit to first action.
**When to use it:** Between plays, backstage, before a critical attempt.
**Expected short-term effect:** Regain composure and external/process focus.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Part of mental skills packages; limited standalone RCTs for branded sequence—supported within broader arousal/attention control evidence.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Breath + attentional narrowing to controllable process cues; interrupt threat-oriented self-talk.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Ken Ravizza applied sport psychology teachings; empirical support largely from related PPR/breath/attention research.
**Potential risks:** Over-centering delaying action; inward focus harming automatic skills if too internal.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Prefer external focus cues for many closed motor skills.
**Source links:** Ravizza applied materials; sport psychology centering descriptions.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Ken Ravizza (popularized centering in sport psychology).

### PETTLEP Imagery
**Protocol name:** PETTLEP Imagery
**Primary purpose:** Mentally rehearse performance with functional equivalence to real execution.
**Problem it targets:** Skill learning limits, travel without practice access, confidence/preparation gaps.
**Typical duration:** 5–15 minutes focused imagery blocks
**Minimum useful duration:** 3–5 minutes of high-quality, specific imagery
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Physical: imagery in similar stance/clothing when possible.
2. Environment: include venue details (or photos/video).
3. Task: image the exact skill/tactics.
4. Timing: real-time speed (not slow-mo unless drilling a cue).
5. Learning: update imagery as skill level changes.
6. Emotion: include typical competition emotions at manageable intensity.
7. Perspective: usually prefer first-person for motor skills; experiment.
8. Pair with physical practice when possible; imagery supplements, rarely replaces, practice.
**When to use it:** Skill acquisition, return-from-injury adjunct, pre-competition activation.
**Expected short-term effect:** Improved readiness and cue familiarity.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Systematic reviews support PETTLEP-informed imagery for sport performance, especially with physical practice.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Functional equivalence / overlapping motor cognitive networks; motivational and cognitive rehearsal.
**Evidence level:** B
**Best supporting research:** Holmes & Collins 2001 PETTLEP model; 2022 systematic reviews on PETTLEP and sport performance.
**Potential risks:** Anxiety amplification if imagery is catastrophe-focused; neglecting physical training.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Active psychosis with imagery conflation; severe PTSD without guidance if imagery triggers trauma.
**Source links:** https://doi.org/10.3390/app12199753 ; Holmes & Collins JASP 2001
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Paul Holmes & Dave Collins (PETTLEP model).

### Implementation Intentions (If–Then Plans)
**Protocol name:** Implementation Intentions (If–Then Plans)
**Primary purpose:** Link situational cues to goal-directed responses to automate follow-through.
**Problem it targets:** Intention–behavior gap, forgotten habits, stalled goals.
**Typical duration:** 5–10 minutes to write plans; seconds to execute
**Minimum useful duration:** One precise if–then for one behavior
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Specify a critical cue: If situation X occurs…
2. Specify a concrete response: …then I will do Y (physical, observable).
3. Make X detectable (time, place, preceding action).
4. Optionally add obstacle if–thens: If craving hits after lunch, then I walk 5 minutes.
5. Review plans daily until automatic.
**When to use it:** Habit formation, health behaviors, study plans, performance cues.
**Expected short-term effect:** Higher likelihood of enacting intended behavior when cue appears.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Meta-analyses show medium effects on goal attainment historically (classic d≈0.65; interpret with heterogeneity/publication-bias caution).
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Delegates control to situational cues; reduces deliberative load; strengthens cue–response links.
**Evidence level:** A
**Best supporting research:** Gollwitzer & Sheeran meta-analytic work on implementation intentions; extensive experimental literature.
**Potential risks:** Overly many plans causing clutter; rigid plans that ignore context—revise.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Not a therapy for clinical depression amotivation alone—combine with care.
**Source links:** Gollwitzer & Sheeran implementation intention meta-analyses.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Peter Gollwitzer.

### Tactical Breath Reset (Short Box-like)
**Protocol name:** Tactical Breath Reset (Short Box-like)
**Primary purpose:** Short box-like reset emphasizing comfort over long holds for performance.
**Problem it targets:** Pre-performance adrenaline with need to stay sharp (not sleepy).
**Typical duration:** 60–120 seconds
**Minimum useful duration:** 4 cycles
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Inhale 4, hold 2–4 (only if comfortable), exhale 4, hold 2–4.
2. Keep eyes open on task-relevant target.
3. End on exhale; execute first action cue.
**When to use it:** Seconds before performance when cyclic sighing feels too downshifting.
**Expected short-term effect:** Composure with alertness preserved better than long meditation.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Practical; evidence inferential from paced breathing + PPR research.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Attentional scaffolding + mild autonomic regulation.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** See box breathing + PPR entries.
**Potential risks:** Holds too long → dizziness.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Hold-intolerant individuals—use exhale-only pacing.
**Source links:** Applied tactical breathing education; Balban 2023 box arm.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Tactical/performance popularization; applied synthesis.

### Task-Switching Buffer
**Protocol name:** Task-Switching Buffer
**Primary purpose:** Reduce switch costs by inserting a brief closure/setup ritual between tasks.
**Problem it targets:** Multitasking residue, errors after abrupt context switches.
**Typical duration:** 1–3 minutes
**Minimum useful duration:** 30–60 seconds
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Write a one-line parked note: next action for the old task.
2. Close unrelated tabs/notifications for the new task.
3. Take 3 slow breaths or one physiological sigh.
4. State the new task goal in one sentence.
5. Start with a 2-minute ignition action.
**When to use it:** Knowledge work, meetings back-to-back, parenting/work switches.
**Expected short-term effect:** Less cognitive residue; cleaner attentional set.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Aligned with task-switching cost research—buffers are practical applications rather than a branded evidence-based therapy.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Reduces attention residue (Leroy); prepares goal representation for new task.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Cognitive psychology of task switching; Sophie Leroy attention residue research. Buffer ritual is applied synthesis.
**Potential risks:** Over-ritualizing; procrastination disguised as buffering.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Emergency contexts requiring instant switch.
**Source links:** Task-switching literature; attention residue research.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Applied synthesis from cognitive psychology findings.

### Focused Work Block + Break (Pomodoro-style)
**Protocol name:** Focused Work Block + Break (Pomodoro-style)
**Primary purpose:** Protect deep focus with time-boxed work and deliberate recovery breaks.
**Problem it targets:** Distractibility, burnout from unbroken screen time.
**Typical duration:** 25 min work / 5 min break (classic) or 50/10
**Minimum useful duration:** 15 focused minutes + 2-minute break
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Choose one task; set timer (25 or personalized).
2. Work until timer; park distractions on a notepad.
3. Break: stand, eyes off near screens, brief walk/breath—no new rabbit holes.
4. After 3–4 cycles, take a longer break (15–30 min).
5. Adjust lengths to your ultradian rhythm.
**When to use it:** Knowledge work, studying, creative execution.
**Expected short-term effect:** Improved subjective focus and completion cadence.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Popular productivity method; empirical literature mixed/limited vs strong marketing—useful as structure, not magic.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Reduces decision fatigue; creates implementation cue from timer; breaks restore attention.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Limited rigorous trials of classic Pomodoro; related evidence from break/attention and timeboxing practices.
**Potential risks:** Timer anxiety; interrupting flow states inappropriately—flex when in deep flow.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Tasks needing long uninterrupted immersion—use longer blocks.
**Source links:** Francesco Cirillo Pomodoro Technique materials; attention/break research adjacency.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Francesco Cirillo (Pomodoro Technique).
