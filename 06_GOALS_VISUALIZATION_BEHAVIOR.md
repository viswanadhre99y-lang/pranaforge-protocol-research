## Manifestation framing rule

### PETTLEP Imagery
**Protocol name:** PETTLEP Imagery
**Primary purpose:** Mentally rehearse performance with functional equivalence to real execution.
**Problem it targets:** Skill learning limits, travel without practice access, confidence/preparation gaps.
**Typical duration:** 5–15 minutes focused imagery blocks
**Minimum useful duration:** 3–5 minutes of high-quality, specific imagery
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Physical: imagery in similar stance/clothing when possible.
2. Environment: include venue details (or photos/video).
3. Task: image the exact skill/tactics.
4. Timing: real-time speed (not slow-mo unless drilling a cue).
5. Learning: update imagery as skill level changes.
6. Emotion: include typical competition emotions at manageable intensity.
7. Perspective: usually prefer first-person for motor skills; experiment.
8. Pair with physical practice when possible; imagery supplements, rarely replaces, practice.
**When to use it:** Skill acquisition, return-from-injury adjunct, pre-competition activation.
**Expected short-term effect:** Improved readiness and cue familiarity.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Systematic reviews support PETTLEP-informed imagery for sport performance, especially with physical practice.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Functional equivalence / overlapping motor cognitive networks; motivational and cognitive rehearsal.
**Evidence level:** B
**Best supporting research:** Holmes & Collins 2001 PETTLEP model; 2022 systematic reviews on PETTLEP and sport performance.
**Potential risks:** Anxiety amplification if imagery is catastrophe-focused; neglecting physical training.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Active psychosis with imagery conflation; severe PTSD without guidance if imagery triggers trauma.
**Source links:** https://doi.org/10.3390/app12199753 ; Holmes & Collins JASP 2001
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Paul Holmes & Dave Collins (PETTLEP model).

### Implementation Intentions (If–Then Plans)
**Protocol name:** Implementation Intentions (If–Then Plans)
**Primary purpose:** Link situational cues to goal-directed responses to automate follow-through.
**Problem it targets:** Intention–behavior gap, forgotten habits, stalled goals.
**Typical duration:** 5–10 minutes to write plans; seconds to execute
**Minimum useful duration:** One precise if–then for one behavior
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Specify a critical cue: If situation X occurs…
2. Specify a concrete response: …then I will do Y (physical, observable).
3. Make X detectable (time, place, preceding action).
4. Optionally add obstacle if–thens: If craving hits after lunch, then I walk 5 minutes.
5. Review plans daily until automatic.
**When to use it:** Habit formation, health behaviors, study plans, performance cues.
**Expected short-term effect:** Higher likelihood of enacting intended behavior when cue appears.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Meta-analyses show medium effects on goal attainment historically (classic d≈0.65; interpret with heterogeneity/publication-bias caution).
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Delegates control to situational cues; reduces deliberative load; strengthens cue–response links.
**Evidence level:** A
**Best supporting research:** Gollwitzer & Sheeran meta-analytic work on implementation intentions; extensive experimental literature.
**Potential risks:** Overly many plans causing clutter; rigid plans that ignore context—revise.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Not a therapy for clinical depression amotivation alone—combine with care.
**Source links:** Gollwitzer & Sheeran implementation intention meta-analyses.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Peter Gollwitzer.

### WOOP (Mental Contrasting + Implementation Intentions)
**Protocol name:** WOOP (Mental Contrasting + Implementation Intentions)
**Primary purpose:** Increase goal attainment by contrasting wished future with real obstacles, then forming if–then plans.
**Problem it targets:** Fantasy-only goal setting that fails on contact with reality; motivation without strategy.
**Typical duration:** 5–15 minutes per goal
**Minimum useful duration:** 5 minutes for one wish
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Wish: choose a meaningful, challenging, feasible wish.
2. Outcome: vividly imagine the best outcome and how it would feel.
3. Obstacle: identify the main inner obstacle (habit, emotion, belief)—be honest.
4. Plan: form If [obstacle], then I will [action].
5. Repeat for key goals; revisit weekly.
**When to use it:** Personal/health/academic/work goals; de-biasing pure positive visualization.
**Expected short-term effect:** Clearer commitment when expectancy is adequate; sometimes decreased commitment if goal is unrealistic (feature, not bug).
**Expected longer-term effect:** MCII meta-analyses show small–moderate benefits for goal attainment; useful low-cost strategy.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Mental contrasting energizes goal pursuit when expectations are sufficient by linking future to present obstacle; II automates obstacle response (Oettingen + Gollwitzer).
**Evidence level:** A
**Best supporting research:** Oettingen mental contrasting; WOOP practicalization; Frontiers Psychology 2021 MCII meta-analysis (g≈0.34; trim-and-fill lower). Health behavior MCII meta-analyses similar.
**Potential risks:** Demotivation if wish is fantasy-only with low expectancy—may correctly reduce pursuit.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Not for acute suicidality/crisis planning—seek professional care.
**Source links:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/full
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Gabriele Oettingen (mental contrasting/WOOP); II with Peter Gollwitzer.

### SMART Goals with Evidence Caveats
**Protocol name:** SMART Goals with Evidence Caveats
**Primary purpose:** Structure goals as Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound—while recognizing SMART is a heuristic, not a magic formula.
**Problem it targets:** Vague goals; also over-constraining exploratory/creative aims.
**Typical duration:** 10–20 minutes planning
**Minimum useful duration:** Rewrite one vague goal into a testable next action
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Write the goal specifically (what/when/where).
2. Define measurement and a check-in date.
3. Reality-check achievability with constraints.
4. Confirm relevance to values (avoid inherited shoulds).
5. Set time bound and first if–then plan.
6. Caveat: for learning/creative goals, pair with process metrics, not only outcome KPIs.
**When to use it:** Project planning, team OKRs translation to personal actions.
**Expected short-term effect:** Clarity and coordination.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Organizational practice support; experimental superiority of SMART acronym vs other frameworks is not strongly established as unique—implementation quality matters more.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Reduces ambiguity; enables feedback; may increase commitment via concreteness.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** SMART popular management heuristic (Doran 1981 often cited). Goal-setting theory (Locke & Latham) has stronger evidence for specific difficult goals + feedback than for the SMART acronym per se.
**Potential risks:** False precision; neglecting autonomy/mastery; demotivating impossible A.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Using SMART to micromanage recovery from depression—prefer values + tiny actions with clinician/coach.
**Source links:** Locke & Latham goal-setting theory reviews; Doran SMART note.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** George T. Doran (SMART popularization); Locke & Latham (goal-setting science).

### Habit Stacking (Routine-Based Cueing)
**Protocol name:** Habit Stacking (Routine-Based Cueing)
**Primary purpose:** Attach a new tiny behavior to an existing reliable routine cue.
**Problem it targets:** Forgetting new habits; weak cues.
**Typical duration:** 2–5 minutes to design; seconds to perform
**Minimum useful duration:** One stack: After [current habit], I will [new habit <2 min].
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Identify a stable daily routine (make coffee, brush teeth).
2. Attach a tiny version of the new habit immediately after.
3. Keep the new habit laughably small initially.
4. Increase only after automaticity grows.
5. Label: popularized by James Clear; scientifically closest to implementation intentions / habit cue research.
**When to use it:** Building keystone micro-habits.
**Expected short-term effect:** Higher consistency if cue is truly stable.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Habit formation depends heavily on repetition in stable contexts; stack type (routine vs time) may matter less than consistent repetition (RCT evidence).
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Cue–behavior association; reduces prospective memory load.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Popular science (Atomic Habits). Underlying science: implementation intentions; Wood/Neal habit research; RCTs on routine- vs time-based cue planning.
**Potential risks:** Stacking onto unstable cues; ego inflation from reading without practicing.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Relying on books alone for clinical behavior change needs.
**Source links:** Habit formation RCTs; Gollwitzer II literature; Clear as popularizer.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Popularized by James Clear; related ideas in behavior analysis and BJ Fogg Tiny Habits.

### Process Visualization (vs Outcome-Only Fantasy)
**Protocol name:** Process Visualization (vs Outcome-Only Fantasy)
**Primary purpose:** Mentally rehearse steps and obstacle handling—not only the trophy moment.
**Problem it targets:** Motivational fantasy without planning; manifestation confusion.
**Typical duration:** 5–10 minutes
**Minimum useful duration:** 2–3 minutes process imagery + one if–then
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Visualize the process: starting, mid-friction, completing the key action.
2. Include realistic obstacles and your coping response.
3. Avoid only imagining applause/outcome (can reduce effort—mental contrasting literature).
4. End by writing one if–then for today's obstacle.
5. Separate layers: (1) psych mechanisms—planning, motivation, arousal; (2) traditional/spiritual meaning-making if personally valued; (3) do NOT treat supernatural causation as scientific.
**When to use it:** Skill goals, training adherence, interview prep.
**Expected short-term effect:** Increased preparedness and intention clarity.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Process imagery and mental contrasting outperform pure positive fantasy for attainment in research programs.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Cognitive simulation supports planning and self-efficacy; outcome fantasy alone may satiate motivation (Oettingen).
**Evidence level:** B
**Best supporting research:** Oettingen mental contrasting vs indulging; sport imagery literature; contrast with unsupported Law of Attraction claims.
**Potential risks:** Confusing visualization with evidence of future events.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Presenting supernatural manifestation as science—see Unsupported Claims file.
**Source links:** Oettingen research program; imagery reviews.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Synthesis of Oettingen + sport/clinical imagery practices.

### Values Compass (Brief ACT/Values Clarification)
**Protocol name:** Values Compass (Brief ACT/Values Clarification)
**Primary purpose:** Clarify valued directions to guide goals when motivation is chaotic.
**Problem it targets:** Goal conflict, borrowed goals, emptiness after achievement.
**Typical duration:** 15–30 minutes
**Minimum useful duration:** 10 minutes picking top 3 values + one action each
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. List candidate values (connection, growth, courage, health, creativity, etc.).
2. Pick 3 that feel chosen (not only imposed).
3. For each, write one 10-minute action this week that expresses it.
4. Use values as compass when emotions pull off course.
**When to use it:** Quarterly reset; career confusion; emotion-driven goal churn.
**Expected short-term effect:** Increased sense of meaning/choice.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Values work is central in ACT; evidence mainly at package level.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Motivated behavior via chosen meaning; reduces pliance-only goals.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** ACT values components; meaning/values psychological research.
**Potential risks:** Intellectual values without action.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Using values talk to avoid concrete help for clinical depression.
**Source links:** ACT values exercises (Hayes/Wilson); values clarification literature.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** ACT community / values clarification traditions.

### Brief Gratitude Listing
**Protocol name:** Brief Gratitude Listing
**Primary purpose:** Shift attention toward valued positives without denying problems.
**Problem it targets:** Negativity bias load, mild low mood, relationship taking-for-granted.
**Typical duration:** 2–5 minutes
**Minimum useful duration:** Write 3 specific specifics
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Write 3 specific things you are grateful for today (details beat generics).
2. Include why it mattered or who helped.
3. Optionally share one with a person (gratitude expression).
4. Do not use to gaslight yourself out of real problems.
**When to use it:** Mild low mood, relationship maintenance, journaling.
**Expected short-term effect:** Small mood bump for some.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Positive psychology interventions show small average wellbeing effects; heterogeneity high; not depression monotherapy.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Attentional deployment toward positives; social bonding when expressed.
**Evidence level:** C
**Best supporting research:** Emmons & McCullough gratitude experiments; positive psychology meta-analyses (small effects).
**Potential risks:** Spiritual bypassing; shame if forced during grief.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** Acute grief/trauma—permission to not be grateful; clinical depression needs proper care.
**Source links:** Gratitude intervention literature (Emmons et al.).
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Research popularized by Robert Emmons & Michael McCullough.

### Tiny Behavioral Activation Step
**Protocol name:** Tiny Behavioral Activation Step
**Primary purpose:** Counter rumination/withdrawal by scheduling valued activity in small doses.
**Problem it targets:** Depressive inactivity loops, rumination, anhedonia spirals.
**Typical duration:** 5–20 minutes activity
**Minimum useful duration:** 2 minutes of the smallest valued action
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Pick a valued domain (connection, mastery, service, body).
2. Define a 2–10 minute action (walk to mailbox, text a friend, wash one dish).
3. Schedule it; do it regardless of motivation (action first).
4. Note mood 0–10 before/after without judgment.
5. Build upward; for clinical depression use full BA with clinician.
**When to use it:** Low mood, rumination, stuckness; as CBT/BA skill excerpt.
**Expected short-term effect:** Often small mood lift; interrupts brooding.
**Expected longer-term effect:** Behavioral activation has strong evidence for depression as a therapy package.
**Mechanism / proposed mechanism:** Increases response-contingent positive reinforcement; breaks avoidance–rumination cycles.
**Evidence level:** A
**Best supporting research:** Behavioral activation RCTs and meta-analyses for depression; BA as efficacious psychotherapy.
**Potential risks:** Pushing manic overactivity; ignoring medical fatigue causes.
**Who should avoid or modify it:** If suicidal or severe depression—urgent professional care. Bipolar: structure with clinician.
**Source links:** BA clinical trial literature; CBT/BA treatment guidelines.
**Original creator/developer, if known:** Behavioral activation traditions (e.g., Martell, Dimidjian, Jacobson lineages).
